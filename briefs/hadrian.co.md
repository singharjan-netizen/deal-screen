# Hadrian — first-pass screen

**Source:** https://hadrian.co (public site only, scraped 3 pages)

## What they build
Hadrian owns and runs precision manufacturing factories in the US that make components "from prototype to production." Its own software platform, **Opus**, runs these factories. Opus reads legacy designs and automates machining and inspection. Hadrian sells in four ways: made-to-order parts, dedicated manufacturing or inspection cells (in Hadrian's plants or at customer sites), whole factories that Hadrian designs and operates for a customer, and outsourced procurement through its **Atlas** supply chain platform. Customers are not named. The site presents Hadrian as an alternative to the "legacy defense industrial base," which implies defense and aerospace buyers.

## Sector and category
Defense and aerospace contract manufacturing with software-driven automation. This is mostly a better version of an existing category, the precision machine shop or contract manufacturer. "Factories-as-a-Service" is the one part that could be a new category.

## Risks and open questions
- **Capital intensity and margins:** Hadrian owns and operates factories and offers to build dedicated facilities for customers. Its economics may look more like a manufacturer than a software company. The site says nothing on who funds Factories-as-a-Service capex.
- **Customer concentration and defense budget exposure:** No customers are named, and the whole pitch is framed around defense reindustrialization. Revenue may depend on a few primes or on government spending cycles.
- **Regulation and compliance:** Defense and aerospace work usually requires AS9100, ITAR registration and CMMC. None are mentioned.
- **Scaling execution:** 147 open roles across 9 locations against 3 listed factories means aggressive expansion. Quality and process control could suffer. Running production lines inside customer-owned facilities adds more complexity.
- **Is Opus the moat or is capacity the moat?** If Opus only runs Hadrian's own plants, the advantage is hard to measure from outside. Atlas routes work to third-party suppliers, which may dilute the automation story and the margin.

## Five questions for the founder
1. How does revenue split across Precision Components, Manufacturing-as-a-Service, Factories-as-a-Service and Atlas, and what is the gross margin for each? Is Atlas revenue mostly pass-through supplier spend?
2. Who are your top three customers, what share of revenue do they represent, and how much comes from primes versus direct government contracts?
3. What does Opus measurably do today? For example: what share of CAM programming and inspection plans are generated automatically from legacy drawings, and how do spindle utilization and first-pass yield compare with a conventional shop?
4. You are hiring in Cherokee, AL and Texarkana, TX, where no factory is listed. Are these planned facilities? What does a factory cost, and for Factories-as-a-Service, does the customer or Hadrian carry the capex?
5. What can a technician trained in 30 days actually do compared with an experienced machinist? What are retention and defect rates for that group, and what are your AS9100, ITAR and CMMC statuses by site?

## Screen verdict
**LOOK CLOSER.** Hadrian fits the defense, aerospace and AI thesis and shows real physical scale (3 factories, 147 open roles). But the site gives no customers, no metrics and no proof that Opus gives it an economic edge over a conventional contract manufacturer.
